## Access and Coverage

- State exactly what was inspected and what was unavailable.
- Survey the whole available source before claiming a full reading.
- Check notes, appendices, figures, tables, textual apparatus, or formal material when they bear on the central claims.
- Distinguish printed pagination from PDF pagination and record any reliability limit.

## Attribution and Reconstruction

For every central claim, mark its status:

- author-explicit;
- author-reported view of another source;
- source-supported reconstruction;
- analyst-supplied charitable repair;
- analyst objection or evaluation;
- project-use proposal.

Do not attribute a charitable repair to the author. When the text supports more than one reading, give the main alternatives and state why one is preferred or why the issue remains underdetermined.

## Genre and Method

- For argument-driven work, reconstruct conclusions, grounds, inferences, objections, and replies.
- For historical or exegetical work, separate textual evidence, interpretation, rival readings, historical context, and present philosophical assessment.
- For phenomenological or descriptive work, separate description, case, conceptual articulation, explanation, and any normative or metaphysical conclusion.
- For formal work, check definitions, assumptions, derivations, interpretation, and scope limits.
- For normative work, identify the normative claim, target, support, bridge principles, scope, and force.
- For case- or thought-experiment-driven work, identify stipulations, background assumptions, intended verdict, modal status, and inferential role.
- For philosophically relevant empirical work, separate method and results from the philosophical role assigned to them; do not let data alone settle a conceptual, normative, metaphysical, or interpretive claim.
- For surveys and overview sources, map topic architecture and the source's organizing narrative without treating that narrative as settled field history.

Do not force premise-conclusion form when it obscures the source's actual method. Still make argumentative responsibility and support relations explicit.

## Concepts and Definitions

- Identify load-bearing terms and the author's own vocabulary.
- Say whether a term is defined, stipulated, inherited, operationalized, exemplified, or left implicit.
- Distinguish a definition from an example, slogan, criterion, consequence, or nearby thesis.
- Record scope, contrast class, necessary or sufficient conditions when claimed, and shifts in meaning.
- Use a terminology bridge before introducing external or project vocabulary.

## Arguments and Interpretive Moves

For each central argument or interpretive move, record:

- provenance;
- conclusion or interpretive claim;
- premises, textual grounds, or explanatory considerations;
- inferential or interpretive link;
- scope, modality, and claimed force;
- hidden assumptions and burden of proof;
- support offered for each important premise;
- alternatives the author considers or excludes.

Then assess separately:

- validity or inferential strength;
- textual adequacy for interpretive claims;
- premise support or explanatory adequacy;
- whether the conclusion is stronger than the grounds warrant;
- whether a narrower conclusion survives.

For each central interpretive claim, record the exact passage and context, edition or translation issue when relevant, interpretive principle, competing reading, textual evidence for and against, historical assumptions, and present philosophical evaluation separately.

## Normative Reasoning

- Identify the normative claim type and its bearer, target, scope, strength, and conditions.
- Reconstruct the reasons, values, principles, duties, permissions, virtues, fittingness claims, or rational requirements offered in support.
- Make explicit any bridge from descriptive, conceptual, phenomenological, evaluative, psychological, or empirical premises to a normative conclusion.
- Track grounding, justification, weight, priority, exclusion, enablement, defeat, demandingness, feasibility, and residual obligation when relevant.
- Distinguish a reported intuition, practice, emotional response, or social pattern from a justification of the normative claim.
- Test counterexamples, conflicts, reversals, and whether the conclusion outruns the reasons supplied.

## Objections and Evaluation

Label each objection as author-raised, source-reported, analyst-generated, or project-generated.

- State the exact target: thesis, premise, inference, definition, interpretation, method, case, or scope condition.
- Distinguish internal or immanent criticism from external criticism.
- Present the strongest textually and dialectically plausible version before evaluating it.
- Record the author's reply, if any, separately from an analyst's repair.
- State the cost of the reply and any residual pressure.

## Evidence and Support

Classify the support used:

- conceptual distinction or analysis;
- thought experiment, case, intuition, or ordinary practice;
- textual or historical evidence;
- formal derivation;
- empirical data;
- linguistic evidence;
- explanatory virtue, theoretical cost, genealogy, or burden shifting.

Attach support to the exact premise, interpretation, objection, reply, or framing claim it is meant to support. Mark unchecked empirical, historical, textual, or secondary-source claims as verification needed.

Support appropriate to philosophical inquiry is plural. Do not rank data above conceptual, textual, formal, phenomenological, historical, or normative support merely because it is empirical; assess whether each kind of support bears on the exact claim at issue.

## Debate Framing and Literature Placement

Identify:

- problem setup and live question;
- rivals, allies, foils, precursors, and sources of machinery as the author presents them;
- standards used to assess those views;
- claimed gap, neglected distinction, unresolved pressure, or mistaken framing;
- contribution type;
- the boundary between the author's narrative and independently verified history.

Place the source relationally only when supported. Do not treat a taxonomy, overview, or shared vocabulary as direct support for a positive thesis.

## Locator Check

Every source-specific analytical claim in a durable report should have a page or stable locator when reliable locators exist. If pagination is unreliable, state that and use cautious section or passage descriptions rather than invented precision.
